Big resolutions ("no more Reels, starting now!") tend to last until the first boring evening. Small structural changes last longer, because they don't depend on how motivated you feel in the moment.

This plan gives you **one small change per day for a week**. Each takes five to fifteen minutes. By day seven, you'll have changed the environment around short-form video so that the scroll is no longer the easiest thing to do — while keeping the apps you actually need.

You don't need any special tools for most of it. Where a tool helps, we'll say so, and offer a free alternative.

## Before you start: one honest minute

Open your phone's screen-time screen (Digital Wellbeing on most Android phones, Screen Time on iPhone) and note two numbers:

1. Your **average daily screen time** this past week.
2. The time spent in the **apps where you watch short videos** — YouTube, Instagram, Facebook, TikTok, Snapchat.

Write them down. No judgment — this is just your starting point. You'll check again on day seven.

## Day 1: Notice, don't change

Today, change nothing about how you use your phone. Instead, every time you find yourself in a short-video feed, ask one question: **"What was I doing right before I opened this?"**

Common answers: waiting in line, avoiding a task, lying in bed, after a stressful message, during a boring part of a meeting. Jot them in a note on your phone.

**Why it works:** Most scrolling isn't a decision — it's a response to a trigger. You can't redirect a trigger you haven't noticed. And as we explain in [why short videos are so hard to stop](/guides/why-short-videos-are-hard-to-stop), it's the design of the feed, not a character flaw, so this is observation, not confession.

## Day 2: Clean up notifications

Go through the notification settings for each short-video app and turn off everything that isn't a direct message from a real person: suggested content, "trending", live videos, "you might like", likes on your posts.

**Why it works:** A large share of sessions start with a notification. Every one you remove is a scroll session that never begins.

## Day 3: Move the icons

Move every short-video app off your home screen into a folder on the second or third page. If your launcher allows it, hide them from the home screen entirely and open them by searching.

Put something you'd rather reach for in their old spot: a book app, a podcast app, a notes app, a language-learning app.

**Why it works:** Habits run on autopilot. A thumb that used to land on Instagram now lands on something else. That extra swipe is a small but real stopping cue.

## Day 4: Set your first limit

Pick the one app that takes the most short-form time from you. Set a limit that's lower than your current use, but not so low that you'll resent it — around **half** of your current daily time is a good start.

- **Free option:** Use Android's Digital Wellbeing app timer or iOS Screen Time. Remember these count all time in the app, including messages.
- **With Reelless (Android):** Set a **daily short-form allowance** instead — for example 15 minutes of Reels or Shorts — which only counts time in the short-video feed, not your DMs or searches.

**Why it works:** A limit creates the stopping cue the feed removed. Deciding it today, when you're calm, means you don't have to decide at midnight.

## Day 5: Separate the feed from the app

Today, deal with the feed specifically:

- **YouTube:** Turn off autoplay. Start from search or Subscriptions instead of Home. See [using YouTube as a search engine](/guides/youtube-as-search-engine).
- **Facebook:** Use Feeds → Friends instead of the home feed.
- **Instagram:** Snooze suggested posts; open DMs from the message icon, not the feed.
- **On Android:** If you want to remove the short-video player completely while keeping the rest of each app, this is where a tool like [Reelless](/features) helps — it closes Reels and Shorts players and covers shelves in your feed.

**Why it works:** Most people don't want to quit the app. They want to quit the endless part. Removing just that part means you're no longer fighting your own phone every time you check a message.

## Day 6: Protect your bedtime

The late-night scroll is the most costly one: it eats sleep, and tired brains scroll more the next day. Today, choose a time — say 30 minutes before you want to be asleep — after which short-video apps are off-limits.

- **Free option:** Bedtime mode in Digital Wellbeing (Android) or Downtime in Screen Time (iPhone).
- **With Reelless:** Create a **Bedtime schedule** that blocks short-form video automatically, and turn on **Strict mode** so changing it requires a waiting period.

Then charge your phone somewhere you can't reach from bed, if you can. An inexpensive alarm clock is one of the best digital-wellbeing purchases there is.

**Why it works:** You're removing the trigger (phone in bed) and adding a rule made in advance.

## Day 7: Review and adjust

Check your screen-time numbers again and compare them to your starting point. Then ask:

1. **What worked best?** Keep it.
2. **What felt too strict?** Loosen it slightly — a rule you keep is better than a perfect rule you abandon.
3. **Where did you slip?** Look at your Day 1 trigger list. Is there a trigger you haven't addressed yet?
4. **What did you do with the time?** Notice it. That's the point of all this.

If your numbers barely moved, that's useful information too. Usually it means one trigger is doing most of the work — often bedtime, or a specific app — and that's where to focus next week.

## After the first week

Habits settle over weeks, not days. A few ideas for keeping momentum:

- **Keep one small rule per app** rather than many complicated ones.
- **Review weekly** for a month. A simple weekly report — Reelless shows one on your phone, or your built-in screen-time screen works fine — keeps you honest without obsessing.
- **Allow some short video, on purpose.** A ten-minute daily allowance you enjoy is better than a total ban you resent.
- **Celebrate streaks**, gently. Days under your limit add up.

## A note on kindness

You'll slip. Everyone does — these feeds are designed by large teams to be hard to put down. A slip doesn't mean the plan failed; it means you found a trigger worth fixing. Be curious, not critical, and adjust.

The goal was never to prove you can resist an infinite feed through strength of will. It's to set up your phone so you don't have to.
