You meant to watch one. It's now forty minutes later, your neck is stiff, and you can't remember a single video you watched. If that's familiar, you're in very good company — and it says much less about your willpower than you might think.

This article explains, in plain language, why short-video feeds are so hard to stop. We've deliberately avoided throwing statistics at you; instead we focus on well-established ideas about how habits and attention work, with links where you can read further. Understanding the mechanics won't stop the scroll by itself, but it does change the question from "what's wrong with me?" to "what's this designed to do, and how do I work around it?"

## The word for it

"Doomscrolling" originally described compulsively scrolling through bad news. It has broadened to mean any long, joyless scrolling session that you'd like to stop but don't. [Wikipedia's article on doomscrolling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doomscrolling) is a good starting point for its history.

Short-video feeds are a particularly strong version of this, for a few reasons that reinforce each other.

## 1. Unpredictable rewards

Psychologists have long studied how the *timing* of rewards shapes behavior. One of the most robust findings is that rewards delivered on an unpredictable schedule — sometimes after one try, sometimes after ten — produce more persistent behavior than rewards delivered every time. This idea, called intermittent or variable reinforcement, is covered in the [reinforcement article on Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement).

A short-video feed is an almost perfect variable reward machine. Most videos are fine. Some are boring. Every so often, one is genuinely hilarious, beautiful or fascinating. You never know which swipe will deliver the good one, so the next swipe always feels like it might.

The same principle is often discussed in relation to slot machines. The anthropologist Natasha Dow Schüll's book *Addiction by Design* describes how machine gambling is engineered around continuous, rapid, unpredictable outcomes — a useful lens for understanding infinite feeds, even though scrolling and gambling are not the same thing.

## 2. No stopping cues

Older media had natural endings. A TV episode finished. A newspaper had a last page. A chapter ended. These endings are **stopping cues**: moments that invite you to decide whether to continue.

Infinite feeds remove them. There's no last video, no "you're all caught up", no end credits. The decision to stop never gets presented to you — you have to manufacture it yourself, usually at the moment you're least equipped to (tired, bored, late at night).

This is why friction and natural endings are so effective. A feed that only contains your friends' posts *ends*. A blocked player *ends*. A daily allowance *ends*. You're no longer relying on producing a stopping cue from nothing.

## 3. Autoplay removes the decision to start

In a regular video app, you choose to start each video. In a short-video feed, the next one is already playing. The default action is to keep watching, and stopping requires effort.

Defaults are enormously powerful. Research on choice architecture — popularized in books like *Nudge* by Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein — shows that people overwhelmingly stick with whatever happens if they do nothing. Autoplay makes "do nothing" mean "keep watching".

## 4. Tiny time units

Each short video is 15 to 60 seconds. "One more" always sounds cheap. You'd hesitate before starting another 45-minute episode at midnight; you won't hesitate before a 20-second clip. But a hundred 20-second decisions add up to the same lost hour — without ever triggering the "is this a good use of my time?" check that a bigger commitment would.

## 5. Personalization that learns fast

Short videos generate a lot of feedback very quickly. Every swipe, pause, rewatch and like tells the recommendation system something. Within a short time, the feed can become uncannily well-matched to whatever holds your attention — which is not always the same as what you'd choose to spend time on.

## 6. It's where your friends are

Finally, short videos live inside apps you need. Reels sits next to your DMs. Shorts sits inside the world's biggest library of tutorials. Spotlight is one tab away from your friends' Snaps. You can't simply avoid the building; the feed is in the lobby.

## So it's not a willpower problem

Put these together — unpredictable rewards, no endings, autoplay, tiny time units, fast personalization, and placement inside essential apps — and you get something that reliably beats in-the-moment willpower. That's not a flaw in you. It's the product working as designed.

The upside of seeing it this way is that it points to solutions that don't rely on willpower at all.

## What actually helps

The most effective changes **restore the missing decision points** or **remove the trigger**, rather than asking you to resist harder:

- **Put stopping cues back.** Daily allowances, timers that actually close the feed, and feeds that end (friends-only) all give you an ending you didn't have to create.
- **Remove autoplay where you can.** Turning off autoplay in long-form video apps restores the decision to start.
- **Add a pause before opening.** Even a few seconds of friction before an app opens turns a reflex into a choice. Many people close the app during that pause.
- **Remove the feed but keep the app.** Blocking the short-video player specifically — as [Reelless does](/features) on Android — separates the parts of an app you need from the part designed to keep you watching.
- **Decide in advance.** Rules made in a calm moment (a schedule, a limit, a strict mode) beat decisions made at midnight.
- **Replace the need.** Most scrolling sessions start from boredom, fatigue, loneliness or avoidance. Having something else ready — a podcast, a message to a friend, a walk — addresses the cause, not the symptom.
- **Be kind to yourself.** Shame tends to make the loop worse: feeling bad leads to more scrolling to feel better. Treat slips as data.
